# Free K10 Test Online: What the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale Asks and How to Read It

If you've searched for a "free K10 test online," you're probably looking for a quick, broad measure of psychological distress — not a diagnosis of any specific condition, but a general sense of how much you're struggling. The Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10) is one of the most widely used tools for exactly that purpose. This article walks through what it is, what the ten questions ask, how to interpret the score, and where a free, private version fits into your routine.

## What the K10 Actually Is

The K10 was developed in the 1990s by Ronald Kessler and colleagues for the US National Health Interview Survey. It's a 10-item self-report questionnaire designed to measure nonspecific psychological distress — the general level of emotional suffering, regardless of whether it meets criteria for a specific disorder.

The K10 is used worldwide in population health surveys, epidemiological research, and clinical settings. It's particularly valued for its ability to identify people who are likely to have a serious mental illness without requiring a full diagnostic interview. Because it measures distress broadly, it captures the overlap between depression, anxiety, and other forms of psychological suffering.

## The Ten Questions, Summarized

Over the past 4 weeks (or past 30 days, depending on the version), how often did you:

1. Feel tired out for no good reason
2. Feel nervous
3. Feel so nervous that nothing could calm you down
4. Feel hopeless
5. Feel restless or fidgety
6. Feel so restless you could not sit still
7. Feel depressed
8. Feel that everything was an effort
9. Feel so sad that nothing could cheer you up
10. Feel worthless

Each answer is scored on a 5-point scale: none of the time = 1, a little of the time = 2, some of the time = 3, most of the time = 4, all of the time = 5. The total ranges from 10 (minimum) to 50 (maximum).

## How to Read the Score

### Score Ranges

The conventional cutoffs (used in many population health surveys):

- **10–15:** Likely to be well (low distress)
- **16–21:** Likely to have a mild mental disorder
- **22–29:** Likely to have a moderate mental disorder
- **30–50:** Likely to have a severe mental disorder

A score of 20 or above is often used as the threshold for likely serious mental illness. But a number alone never tells the whole story — the K10 measures distress, not a specific diagnosis, and the same score can mean different things for different people.

### What the Score Doesn't Tell You

The K10 is a measure of general distress, not a diagnostic tool. A high score doesn't tell you whether you're experiencing depression, anxiety, PTSD, or another condition — it just tells you that distress is present. A low score doesn't mean everything is fine; it means distress was low during the recall period.

Not every "free K10 test online" is built the same. A respectful implementation should:

## Tracking Over Time

The K10 is most useful when taken periodically — monthly is a common interval, matching the 30-day recall window. A single score is a snapshot; a trendline is information. If you're logging mood anyway, pairing that with a periodic screening gives you and any clinician you work with a richer picture.

## The Strengths of the K10

### It's Broad

Unlike condition-specific screens (PHQ-9 for depression, GAD-7 for anxiety), the K10 captures general distress. This makes it useful when you're not sure what's going on — when you just know you're struggling but don't know what to call it.

### It's Well-Validated

The K10 has been used in major national and international health surveys, giving it a strong evidence base. Its cutoffs are well-established, and it performs well at identifying people who are likely to have a serious mental illness.

### It's Short

Ten questions is brief enough to complete in a few minutes, making it practical for routine use.

## The Limitations of the K10

### It Doesn't Diagnose

The K10 measures distress, not disorders. A high score warrants further evaluation by a professional to determine what's going on.

### It Uses a 30-Day Window

The K10 asks about the past month. If you had a good month but have been struggling for longer, the score may underrepresent your experience.

### It Doesn't Assess Risk

The K10 doesn't include specific items about self-harm or suicide. If you're experiencing thoughts of self-harm, the K10 won't flag this specifically — and you should seek help regardless of your score.
