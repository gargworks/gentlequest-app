# Free DASS-21 Test Online: What the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale Asks and How to Read It

If you've searched for a "free DASS-21 test online," you're probably looking for a single tool that can help you check in on three related but distinct emotional states — depression, anxiety, and stress — without completing three separate questionnaires. The Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) is designed for exactly that. This article walks through what it is, what it measures, how to interpret the scores, and where a free, private version fits into your routine.

## What the DASS-21 Actually Is

The DASS-21 is a 21-item self-report questionnaire developed by Lovibond and Lovibond. It's a shortened version of the original 42-item DASS. Each of the 21 items maps to one of three subscales: depression, anxiety, or stress. Seven items per subscale, rated on a 0–3 frequency scale over the past week.

The DASS-21 was designed to measure the negative emotional states of depression, anxiety, and stress, and to do so in a way that distinguishes between them. This is useful because depression and anxiety frequently co-occur, and their symptoms can overlap. The DASS-21 helps parse which is which — or whether both are present.

Because it's relatively short, public-domain, and scored on a simple scale, it translates well to a digital format. That's why "free DASS-21 test online" is a common search.

## What the 21 Items Measure

### Depression Subscale (7 items)

These items assess symptoms like low mood, hopelessness, devaluation of life, self-deprecation, lack of interest, anhedonia, and inertia. They map to the core features of depression but focus on the past week, not a longer period.

### Anxiety Subscale (7 items)

These items assess physical arousal, autonomic arousal, skeletal muscle effects, situational anxiety, and subjective experience of anxious affect. They capture the physical and experiential components of anxiety.

### Stress Subscale (7 items)

These items assess difficulty relaxing, nervous arousal, being easily upset/agitated, irritability/over-reactivity, and impatience. Stress here refers to a state of persistent tension and over-arousal — not a specific trigger, but the felt sense of being overloaded.

## How to Read the Scores

Each subscale is scored by summing the seven items (0–21 per subscale) and then doubling the result (to make it comparable to the full 42-item DASS, which ranges 0–42 per subscale). The conventional severity ranges:

### Depression

- **0–9:** Normal
- **10–13:** Mild
- **14–20:** Moderate
- **21–27:** Severe
- **28+:** Extremely severe

### Anxiety

- **0–7:** Normal
- **8–9:** Mild
- **10–14:** Moderate
- **15–19:** Severe
- **20+:** Extremely severe

### Stress

- **0–14:** Normal
- **15–18:** Mild
- **19–25:** Moderate
- **26–33:** Severe
- **34+:** Extremely severe

A score in the moderate or higher range on any subscale is a signal that further evaluation may be warranted. But a number alone never tells the whole story — the pattern across subscales, and your individual circumstances, matter more than any single total.

## Tracking Over Time

The DASS-21 is most useful when taken periodically — weekly is the standard interval, matching the recall window. A single score is a snapshot; a trendline is information. If you're logging mood anyway, pairing that with a periodic screening gives you and any clinician you work with a richer picture.

## The Limitations of the DASS-21

### It's a Screen, Not a Diagnosis

The DASS-21 measures symptoms, not disorders. A high depression score doesn't mean you have major depressive disorder — it means you're experiencing depressive symptoms. The distinction matters, and it's why clinical evaluation by a professional is essential for diagnosis.

### It Captures a Narrow Time Window

The DASS-21 asks about the past week. If you had a good week but have been struggling for months, the score may underrepresent your experience. Conversely, a bad week can inflate the score beyond your baseline.

### It Doesn't Assess All Conditions

The DASS-21 covers depression, anxiety, and stress — but not PTSD, OCD, bipolar disorder, eating disorders, or other conditions that may co-occur with or mimic these states. A normal DASS-21 score doesn't rule out other conditions.
