# The Comprehensive Safety Plan Guide: Preparing for Your Hardest Moments

A safety plan is a written, personalized plan for getting through a mental health crisis. It's not a document you create when you're in crisis — it's something you build when you're relatively stable, so it's ready when you need it. A good safety plan is simple, specific, and accessible. It doesn't prevent crises, but it gives you a roadmap for navigating them. This guide is a comprehensive resource for understanding, creating, and using a safety plan.

## What Is a Safety Plan?

### The Definition

A safety plan is a structured document that outlines the steps you'll take when you're experiencing a mental health crisis, particularly when you're having thoughts of self-harm or suicide. It's a personalized set of strategies, contacts, and reminders that you can turn to when your thinking is compromised by intense emotion.

### Why It Works

During a crisis, your brain's threat-detection system is fully activated. Your thinking narrows, your ability to problem-solve decreases, and your perspective shrinks to the immediate moment. In this state, you can't reliably come up with a plan — you need one that already exists.

A safety plan works because:

- **It's pre-made:** You don't have to think clearly to use it; you just have to follow it.
- **It's personalized:** It contains your specific triggers, coping strategies, and contacts — not generic advice.
- **It's incremental:** It moves from self-help steps to seeking help, so you start with the least intensive option and escalate as needed.
- **It's external:** It exists outside your overwhelmed mind, on paper or in an app, where you can see it even when your thinking is compromised.

### Who Needs a Safety Plan?

Anyone who experiences mental health crises can benefit from a safety plan. It's particularly important for people who:

- Have had thoughts of self-harm or suicide
- Experience severe anxiety or panic attacks
- Have a history of mental health crises
- Are going through a particularly difficult period
- Have been recently discharged from mental health treatment

You don't need to be in crisis to create a safety plan. In fact, the best time to create one is when you're feeling relatively stable.

## The Components of a Safety Plan

A comprehensive safety plan, based on the Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention, includes six key components:

### 1. Warning Signs

List the thoughts, feelings, behaviors, or situations that signal you're approaching a crisis. These are your personal early warning system.

Examples:
- Thoughts: "I start thinking that things will never get better"
- Feelings: "I feel trapped, hopeless, or overwhelmingly anxious"
- Behaviors: "I stop answering texts, I stop eating, I isolate"
- Situations: "After a fight with my family, during financial stress, around anniversaries of loss"

The more specific you can be, the better. These warning signs help you recognize a crisis building before it peaks, giving you time to use your coping strategies.

### 2. Internal Coping Strategies

List things you can do on your own to distract or calm yourself. These are strategies that don't require anyone else — they're your first line of defense.

Examples:
- Breathing exercises (box breathing, extended exhale)
- Grounding techniques (5-4-3-2-1)
- Physical activity (walk, run, stretch)
- Listening to specific music
- Watching a specific show
- Progressive muscle relaxation
- Journaling

Our [journaling guide](/articles/journal-app-no-ads) covers journaling as a coping tool.

### 3. Social Contacts for Distraction

List people and places that can provide distraction and connection without necessarily knowing you're in crisis. These are lower-intensity social strategies.

Examples:
- "Call my friend [name] and talk about something unrelated"
- "Go to [specific coffee shop] and be around people"
- "Text [name] and ask about their day"
- "Join an online community and participate"

The key is that these contacts provide connection and distraction without requiring you to explain your crisis. Sometimes just being around people or having a normal conversation is enough to reduce the intensity.

### 4. People to Ask for Help

List specific people you can reach out to and directly ask for help. These are your closer contacts — people who know about your mental health and can provide support.

Examples:
- "Call my partner at [number]"
- "Text my sibling at [number]"
- "Call my therapist at [number]"
- "Contact my support group leader at [number]"

Include the actual phone numbers. During a crisis, looking up numbers is a barrier. Having them right there in the plan removes that barrier.

### 5. Professionals and Agencies

List professional resources, including crisis lines, your therapist, your doctor, and emergency services.

Examples:
- "Call or text 988 (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline)"
- "Text HOME to 741741 (Crisis Text Line)"
- "Call my therapist at [number]"
- "Call my psychiatrist at [number]"
- "Go to [local emergency room]"
- "Call [local crisis center] at [number]"

These are your highest-intensity resources. The plan escalates from self-help to social contacts to professional help to emergency services.

### 6. Making the Environment Safe

List steps to make your immediate environment safer during a crisis. This is particularly important if you have thoughts of self-harm.

Examples:
- "Remove [specific items] from my immediate area"
- "Give [medication] to [trusted person] for safekeeping"
- "Go to a safe location (specific place)"
- "Ask [person] to stay with me"

This step removes the means for impulsive self-harm during the crisis window, giving your coping strategies time to work.

## How to Create a Safety Plan

### Do It When You're Stable

The most important rule: create your safety plan when you're feeling relatively calm and clear-headed. Don't wait for a crisis to make a plan. If you're currently in crisis, reach out for help immediately: call or text 988, or text HOME to 741741.

### Be Specific

Vague plans don't work in crisis. "Call a friend" is useless if you can't remember which friend or their number. "Call Sarah at 555-0123" is actionable even when your thinking is compromised.

### Be Realistic

Include strategies you'll actually use. If you hate going for walks, don't list "go for a walk" as a coping strategy. If you're not close to your family, don't list them as contacts. The plan should reflect your real life, not an idealized version.

### Involve Others

If possible, involve a trusted person in creating your plan. They can help you identify warning signs you might miss and suggest strategies you might not think of. They'll also know the plan exists, which means they can remind you to use it.

### Keep It Accessible

Your safety plan is useless if you can't find it during a crisis. Keep it:

- In an app on your phone (always with you)
- On paper in a specific, known location
- Saved as a phone lock screen or wallpaper
- Shared with a trusted person who can remind you

### Review and Update

Your life changes, and your safety plan should change with it. Review it every few months. Update contacts if numbers change. Add new coping strategies you've discovered. Remove things that don't work.

## Using a Safety Plan During a Crisis

### Start at the Top

When you notice your warning signs, start at the top of the plan. Try your internal coping strategies first. If they're not enough, move to social contacts. If those aren't enough, reach out to people for help. If that's not enough, contact professionals. If that's not enough, go to emergency services.

The plan is a ladder — you start at the bottom (self-help) and climb up (professional help) as needed. You don't have to start at the top.

### Don't Skip Steps, But Don't Get Stuck

Move through the steps at your own pace, but don't get stuck on one step if it's not working. If breathing exercises aren't helping after 5 minutes, move to social contacts. If a friend isn't available, move to the next contact. The goal is to keep moving through the plan until you find something that helps.

### Use the Plan Before You Need It

The best use of a safety plan is before a full crisis — when you notice your warning signs but haven't yet spiraled. Using coping strategies at the warning-sign stage can prevent the crisis from developing.

### Tell Someone You're Using It

If you're using your safety plan, tell someone. "I'm using my safety plan right now" is a clear, simple message that tells a trusted person you need support without requiring you to explain everything.

## Safety Plans for Specific Situations

### Suicidal Thoughts

If you're having thoughts of suicide, your safety plan is critical. The "making the environment safe" step is particularly important — removing means during the crisis window saves lives. The crisis line numbers (988, 741741) should be prominent in your plan. If you're in immediate danger, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency room.

### Panic Attacks

For panic attacks, the safety plan focuses on in-the-moment coping: breathing techniques, grounding, and cognitive reframing. The plan might include a specific script to read ("This is a panic attack. It's not dangerous. It will pass.") and a person to call who can talk you through it. Our [panic attacks guide](/articles/panic-attack-app-free) covers panic management.

### Severe Anxiety

For severe anxiety episodes, the safety plan includes grounding techniques, breathing, and contacts who can help you reorient. It might also include reminders of what's helped before ("Last time I felt this way, breathing and a walk helped"). Our [anxiety guide](/articles/anxiety-app-no-ads) covers anxiety management.

### Depression Crises

For depression crises — periods of severe hopelessness or despair — the safety plan includes behavioral activation steps (small actions that break the inertia), contacts who can provide connection, and professional resources. Our [depression guide](/articles/depression-app-no-ads) covers depression management.

### PTSD Episodes

For PTSD flashbacks or dissociation, the safety plan includes grounding techniques specific to trauma (orienting to the present, using sensory anchors), contacts who understand trauma, and professional resources. Our [CPTSD support app guide](/articles/cptsd-support-app) and [PTSD app guide](/articles/ptsd-app-no-subscription) cover PTSD management.

## The Role of Privacy in Safety Planning

Your safety plan contains your most sensitive information — your warning signs, your coping strategies, your contacts, and your crisis resources. This information should be:

- Accessible to you at all times
- Private from everyone else unless you choose to share it
- Stored on your device, not on a server
- Never used to profile you, deny you services, or shared with third parties

A [private mental health app](/articles/private-mental-health-app) that stores your safety plan on your device, doesn't track you, and doesn't require an account is the right tool for this. Your safety plan should be the most private thing on your phone.

## When to Seek Immediate Help

A safety plan is a tool, not a guarantee. If you're in immediate danger or having active thoughts of suicide with a plan and intent, do not rely on your safety plan alone. Seek immediate help:

- **Call or text 988** (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline in the US)
- **Text HOME to 741741** (Crisis Text Line)
- **Call 911** or go to your nearest emergency room
- **Reach out to someone you trust** and tell them you need help right now

If you are outside the US, find your local crisis line. The International Association for Suicide Prevention (IASP) maintains a directory of crisis centers worldwide.

## A Note on What This Isn't

This guide is for general information and education only. It's not a substitute for professional mental health care, and it's not a crisis intervention. If you're currently in crisis or having thoughts of self-harm, please reach out for help immediately: in the US, call or text 988, or text HOME to 741741. A safety plan is a tool to support you between and during crises, but it works best alongside professional treatment.

This article is not a diagnosis. If you're struggling, please see a qualified mental health professional. If you're in crisis right now, please reach out for immediate help.
