## Body (600 words)

Every habit guide assumes a good week. The routines, the systems, the protocols — they're all designed when life is stable, motivation is available, and nothing is on fire. "Wake up at 6, meditate for ten minutes, journal three pages, exercise, eat well." Easy. On a good week.

But the test of a habit isn't whether you do it on a good week. It's whether it survives the bad one. The week the deadline moves up. The week someone gets sick. The week the argument happens. The week you sleep poorly, eat badly, and feel like the ground is shifting under you.

That week, your good-week habits are nowhere to be found. The ten-minute meditation is impossible. The three-page journal entry is laughable. The exercise routine doesn't happen. And when the bad week ends, you look back and think: "I failed. I need to start over." So you start over — at full intensity — and the cycle repeats.

The fix is to design for the floor, not the ceiling. Before the bad week arrives, while you're still in a good week, you create the minimum viable version of every habit. The version you can do at midnight, exhausted, upset, with zero motivation. Not the ideal version — the survival version.

**Principle 1: Shrink before stress hits.**
Don't wait for a bad week to reduce your habit. Pre-shrink it now. If your habit is a five-prompt reflection, define the one-prompt version right now. If your habit is a thirty-minute walk, define the five-minute version now. The floor version should be ready before you need it, not designed in the middle of a crisis.

**Principle 2: Remove all optional steps.**
A habit with optional parts will shed them under stress, then collapse. "I'll journal, and maybe meditate, and maybe stretch" becomes "I'll journal" becomes "I'll do it tomorrow" becomes nothing. The floor version has zero optional steps. One action. One prompt. One number. No maybe.

**Principle 3: Pre-write the bad-day prompt.**
Write down the exact prompt you'll use on a bad day. Not "I'll figure it out when it happens" — write it now, while you're calm. "On a bad day, I will log one mood rating. That counts as a complete entry." Put it somewhere visible. When the bad day arrives, you don't have to think. You just execute the pre-written plan.

The mindset shift: "I did the minimum" is success, not failure. On a bad week, doing the floor version of your habit is an extraordinary achievement. It means the habit survived. It means you showed up when showing up was hard. It means the streak — not a streak of perfection, but a streak of existence — continued.

Here's what happens when you design for the floor. The bad week comes. You do the minimum version of your habits. They survive. The bad week ends. You don't have to "start over" because you never stopped. You just downshifted temporarily, and now you can upshift again. The habit is intact. The identity is intact. The practice continues.

Compare this to the ceiling-design approach. The bad week comes. You do nothing. The habit dies. The bad week ends. You have to rebuild from scratch, with the added weight of guilt and the evidence that you "failed." The restart takes more energy than the floor version ever would have.

Design for the floor. The ceiling will take care of itself on good weeks. The floor is what carries you through the bad ones. And the bad ones are when it matters most.

This week, define the floor version of your most important habit. Write it down. Make it laughably small. Then — on your next bad day — do that version and count it as a win. Because it is.
