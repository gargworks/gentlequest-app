## Body (600 words)

Here's a pattern I see in almost every mood tracking dataset: the bad days are overrepresented. Not because there are more bad days — but because people only open the app when they feel bad.

A 3 out of 10 triggers an entry. A 7 out of 10 doesn't. The logic is understandable: when you feel bad, you want to record it, process it, make it visible. When you feel fine, you don't think about mood tracking at all. Fine doesn't trigger reflection. Fine doesn't prompt you to open an app.

The result is a one-sided archive. After three months, your mood log looks like a chronicle of distress — 3s, 4s, occasional 5s, and very few entries above 6. When you review it, the data confirms a depressing narrative: "My life is mostly bad days." But that's not what happened. What happened is you only recorded the bad ones.

This is selection bias, and it's one of the most common distortions in self-tracking. The dataset doesn't reflect reality — it reflects when you chose to measure. And you chose to measure when things were hard.

The fix is simple but requires intention: log the good days too. Not with forced gratitude or manufactured positivity — with the same neutral observation you bring to the bad days. A 7 is data. An 8 is data. A "pretty good Tuesday" is data. And that data is essential, because without it, the bad days have no contrast. They look like the whole picture instead of part of it.

Here's the protocol: the "good day minimum." Any day you'd rate 7 or above, you log it. No exceptions, no "I'll do it later," no "it wasn't that special." If it was a 7, it gets an entry. The entry can be one word — "good" — or one sentence. The content doesn't matter. The act of recording does.

Why does this matter? Three reasons.

First, it corrects the narrative. When your dataset includes the 7s and 8s, the 3s become what they actually are: part of a range, not the whole story. A month with five 3s and fifteen 7s looks very different from a month with five 3s and nothing else. The same bad days, but a completely different picture.

Second, it creates a resource. On a future bad day — a real 2 or 3 — you can look back at the good days and see that they existed. Not as an abstract "things will get better" reassurance, but as concrete evidence from your own life. "Last Tuesday was an 8. I can get there again." The archive becomes a comfort tool, not just a distress log.

Third, it trains attention. When you know you're supposed to log good days, you start noticing them. The 7 that you would have let pass unremarked now gets a moment of acknowledgment. You're not manufacturing happiness — you're catching it. The act of recording a good day amplifies it slightly, because you're pausing to recognize it.

The resistance to this is usually some version of "I don't want to brag" or "it wasn't that good." Neither objection holds up. You're not publishing this. It's private. And "not that good" is exactly the point — a 7 isn't a peak experience. It's a decent day. Decent days are the majority of life, and they deserve to be in the record.

So this week, add one rule: if the day is a 7 or above, log it. That's it. One entry on every good day, no matter how ordinary. The bad days will take care of themselves — you're already logging those. The good days need a nudge.

Your future self, looking back at this data during a hard week, will thank you for every 7 you recorded. Don't make them search for evidence that good days existed. Give them the data.
