## Body (600 words)

Here's the thing about starting a new habit: we always start too big.

You decide to journal. So you buy a notebook, set aside twenty minutes each morning, and write three pages on day one. By day four, the notebook is on your nightstand, untouched. You feel guilty. You close the notebook and put it in a drawer. The habit is dead.

The problem isn't your willpower. The problem is the size. You designed a habit for your most motivated self — the version of you that exists on day one, full of energy and optimism. But that version of you doesn't show up every day. The version that shows up on day seven is tired, busy, and not in the mood for three pages of anything.

The fix is simple but uncomfortable: make the habit so small that your worst day can accommodate it.

Instead of three pages, write one sentence. Instead of twenty minutes, spend ninety seconds. Instead of a perfect morning routine, do one thing after you pour your coffee. The habit should feel almost too easy — small enough that skipping it would feel stranger than doing it.

This feels wrong. We've been taught that small efforts don't count. That if you're not going all in, why bother. But the evidence points the other direction. Habits that survive the first two weeks tend to survive for months. Habits that don't survive week one never had a chance. The variable that matters most isn't intensity — it's survival rate.

Think of it like planting a seed. You don't judge a seed by how tall it is on day one. You judge it by whether it's still alive on day thirty. A tiny habit that's still going on day thirty has compound interest working for it. A big habit that died on day four has nothing.

So here's the experiment for this week: pick one habit. Make it the smallest version you can think of. Not the ideal version — the minimum version. The version you could do at midnight after a terrible day. Write it down. Do it every day for seven days. Don't expand it. Don't add to it. Don't improve it. Just keep it alive.

If you're not sure what to pick, try this: once a day, rate your mood from 1 to 10. That's the whole habit. One number. Ten seconds. You can do it in line at the grocery store. You can do it in bed. You can do it while waiting for a meeting to start.

The point isn't the number. The point is the act of showing up. The point is proving to yourself that you can do something small, consistently, without turning it into a performance. Once you've proven that, everything else gets easier. Not because you've built discipline — but because you've built trust. Trust that you'll show up. Trust that small counts. Trust that the floor is enough.

That's what The Gentle Quest is about. Not transformation. Not optimization. Just small experiments, repeated quietly, that add up to something real over time.

Welcome. Let's see what seven days of one number can do.
