## Body (600 words)

It's 10 PM. Your brain is done. You've made thousands of decisions since morning. Your willpower is at zero. And someone — a wellness blog, a productivity podcast, a well-meaning friend — suggests you "reflect on your day."

You want to throw something.

The problem isn't reflection. Reflection is valuable. The problem is that most reflection prompts are designed for a morning brain, not an evening brain. "What did I learn today?" requires synthesis. "What am I grateful for?" requires emotional access. "How could I have done better?" requires self-evaluation. All of these demand cognitive energy that doesn't exist at 10 PM.

Evening reflection needs to be designed for a depleted brain. Low-stakes, specific, and answerable in under thirty seconds per prompt. Not a journal page. A checklist with feelings.

Here's a five-part evening check-in designed for the tired mind:

**Prompt 1: "What was one small thing that went slightly better than expected?"**
This is the key prompt. "Better than expected" is a low bar — it doesn't require the day to be good, just slightly better than you feared. Maybe the meeting wasn't as bad as you thought. Maybe the commute was five minutes shorter. Maybe lunch was decent. The specificity matters: "one small thing" is findable even on a bad day.

**Prompt 2: "Rate your energy from 1 to 5."**
Not your mood — your energy. Energy is easier to assess at night because your body tells you directly. A number. No explanation needed. This creates an energy log alongside your mood log, and over time you'll see how energy and mood interact.

**Prompt 3: "Name one emotion you felt today."**
One word. Not a paragraph. "Frustrated." "Content." "Anxious." "Relieved." If you can only think of "tired," that counts. The goal is to maintain the emotional granularity practice from Issue 03, not to write an essay.

**Prompt 4: "Is there anything on your mind right now?"**
One sentence maximum. This is a brain dump — not a resolution. If something is looping in your head, write it here and leave it. The prompt is a container, not a to-do list. "I'm worried about tomorrow's presentation." Done. Logged. Released for the night.

**Prompt 5: "What's one thing you're letting go of for tonight?"**
This is the release prompt. Something from today — a mistake, an awkward moment, an unfinished task — that you're setting down until tomorrow. Not forever. Just for tonight. The act of naming what you're releasing creates a psychological boundary between the day and the night.

Five prompts. Under three minutes. No creative energy required. Each prompt is concrete enough that a tired brain can answer it on autopilot. The sequence closes the mental loop of the day without turning bedtime into a performance review.

The trick is consistency, not depth. Do the same five prompts every night. Predictability reduces friction — your brain learns the sequence and starts answering before you've finished reading the question. After two weeks, the check-in takes ninety seconds. After a month, it feels like brushing your teeth. Boring, automatic, and somehow essential.

The other trick: keep your phone within arm's reach. Don't require getting up, finding a notebook, or sitting at a desk. The check-in should happen wherever you already are at 10 PM — bed, couch, kitchen counter. Friction is the enemy of evening habits. Eliminate every step you can.

This week, try the five-part check-in. If five feels like too many, do three. If three feels like too many, do one — the "better than expected" prompt alone is worth it. The goal is not a perfect reflection practice. The goal is a practice that survives contact with a tired brain.

## This Week's Experiment
Each evening, do the five-prompt check-in. If you're too tired for all five, do just one: "What went slightly better than expected?" One answer. That's a complete entry.
