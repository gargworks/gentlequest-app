## Body (600 words)

Your journal contains the thoughts you wouldn't say out loud. The doubts, the fears, the half-formed ideas, the honest assessments of yourself and others. This is the most private data you generate — more private than your banking, more private than your search history, more private than your messages.

So where does it live?

If you use a cloud-based journaling app, the answer is: on someone else's server. Your most private thoughts are stored on a computer owned by a company, accessed via an internet connection, protected by a password you probably reuse, and governed by a terms of service you definitely didn't read.

This isn't paranoia. It's architecture. Cloud journaling means your entries exist on infrastructure you don't control. If the company has a data breach, your entries are exposed. If the company changes its terms, your entries might be analyzed. If the company shuts down, your entries might disappear. If you lose internet access, you can't read your own thoughts.

The alternative is local-first. Your entries live on your device — encrypted, accessible offline, never sent to a server unless you explicitly choose. You don't need an account. You don't need a login. You don't need permission to read what you wrote.

Here's what local-first actually means, in practical terms:

**Your entries are stored on your device.** Not in the cloud. On your phone, your laptop, your tablet. The data lives where you live.

**Encryption is on by default.** Your entries are encrypted at rest. Even if someone gets physical access to your device, they can't read your journal without your key.

**No account required.** You don't create an account, provide an email, or share any personal information. The app doesn't know who you are. It just stores your data.

**Offline by default.** You can read, write, and review your entries without an internet connection. Airplane mode, no signal, remote cabin — your journal works.

**Export anytime.** Your data is yours. You can export it in a standard format at any time. If you switch apps, you take your entries with you. No lock-in.

**Optional sync.** If you want your entries on multiple devices, sync is available — but it's end-to-end encrypted and opt-in. The default is local-only.

The tradeoffs are real but manageable. Multi-device sync requires setup. You're responsible for backups (though a good app automates this). There are no "social" features — no sharing, no community, no commenting. These aren't bugs; they're features. A journal that can't be shared is a journal you can be honest in.

How to evaluate any journaling app's privacy: ask three questions. Where is my data stored? (Look for "on-device" or "local.") What does the terms of service say about my content? (Look for "we do not access" or "end-to-end encrypted.") Is there an offline mode? (If no, your data is cloud-dependent.)

The deeper point is about what journaling is for. Journaling works because it's honest. Honesty requires safety. Safety requires knowing that your words won't be read by anyone except you, ever, unless you choose. Cloud journaling introduces a sliver of doubt — a tiny, background awareness that your words are on a server, accessible to someone else. That doubt changes what you write. You self-censor. You soften. You skip the entry that's too raw.

Local-first journaling removes that doubt. The words never leave your device. The only person who reads them is you. That safety is the foundation of honest reflection. Without it, you're not journaling — you're composing for an audience you hope never arrives.

Your thoughts deserve the same privacy as your home. Local-first is the lock on the door.
