## Body (600 words)

You log a bad mood. A 3 out of 10. Then you feel bad about having a bad mood. Then you feel worse for not logging yesterday. Then you feel guilty for feeling guilty. Then you close the app and don't open it for a week.

This is the guilt spiral. It's the number one reason people abandon mood tracking, journaling, and most wellness practices. And it's entirely preventable — not by trying harder, but by changing the frame.

The spiral starts with a single error: treating a mood entry as an evaluation instead of an observation. When you log a 3, you're not saying "I am a 3." You're saying "right now, in this moment, my internal weather is a 3." That's it. It's a data point, not a verdict. But the brain doesn't naturally make that distinction. It hears "3" and translates it to "I'm failing."

The first reframing technique is naming. When you log a low mood, name what's happening without judgment. "I'm logging a 3. That's data, not a grade." Say it out loud if you need to. The act of naming the entry as neutral data interrupts the automatic evaluation. It sounds silly. It works.

The second technique is neutralizing. Treat your mood log like a weather report, not a report card. A meteorologist doesn't feel personally responsible for a rainy day. They just record it. You're the meteorologist of your own mind. The 3 isn't your fault. It's a condition. Conditions change. Your job is to record them, not to fix them.

The third technique is normalizing. A 3 is not abnormal. Everyone has 3s. If you tracked a hundred people's moods for a month, you'd see a distribution — some 8s, some 5s, some 3s, occasionally a 2. The 3 is part of the normal range, not an outlier that means something is wrong with you. When you normalize the low numbers, they lose their power to trigger shame.

There's also the skip guilt — the feeling that missing a day has ruined the dataset. It hasn't. A mood log with a gap is still a mood log. The gap is data too. It probably means the day was busy or difficult or you simply forgot. That's human. The dataset isn't ruined. It's real. Real data has gaps.

The most important rule: missing a day doesn't reset anything meaningful. There's no streak to break. There's no score to lose. There's no judgment from the app, from the data, or from anyone else. The only judgment comes from you, and you can choose to drop it.

Here's what happens when you apply these three techniques consistently. The guilt spiral shortens. Instead of lasting three days, it lasts three minutes. Instead of abandoning the practice, you log the next day. Instead of dreading the app, you open it with neutral curiosity. The mood entries become what they were always supposed to be: a quiet record of your inner weather, taken at intervals, that accumulates into a picture of your patterns over time.

The goal of mood tracking was never to have all good numbers. The goal was to have data — all of it, the 3s and the 8s and the 5s — so you can see the full picture. Guilt makes that impossible by driving you away from logging the hard days. And the hard days are the most important ones to log. They're the ones that reveal the patterns. They're the ones that, months later, you look back at and think: "Oh. That was the week everything was hard. And I got through it."

Log the 3. Don't judge it. Don't explain it. Don't fix it. Just record it. The recording is the practice. The practice is enough.

## This Week's Experiment
Log your mood every day this week — including the bad days. Each time you log a low number, say to yourself: "That's data, not a verdict." If you skip a day, log the next day without apology.
