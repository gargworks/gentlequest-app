## Body (600 words)

Before coffee. Before email. Before the phone, the news, the group chat, the calendar. Before any of it: ninety seconds.

Three questions. Not meditation. Not gratitude. Not a journal page. A quick systems diagnostic for your mind — the same way you'd check the weather before choosing what to wear.

**Question 1: "How am I feeling right now?"**
One word or one number. Not an analysis — a reading. "Tired." "Anxious." "7." "Fine." The point is to take a measurement before the day starts pulling you in directions. Your pre-day mood is a baseline. Everything that happens after is measured against it. If you start at a 5 and end at a 3, that's useful data. If you start at a 5 and end at a 7, that's also useful data. But you can't measure the change without the baseline, and the baseline has to be taken before the inputs start.

**Question 2: "What's one thing I'm approaching today?"**
Not "what do I have to do" — what are you approaching? The framing matters. "Approaching" implies agency. "Have to do" implies obligation. One thing. Not a list — one. "I'm approaching the presentation." "I'm approaching the conversation with my manager." "I'm approaching the workout." Naming what you're approaching creates a focal point. The day will have many events, but this is the one you're naming as significant. It tells your mind where to direct its preparation energy.

**Question 3: "What would make today slightly better?"**
Not "great." Not "perfect." Slightly better. One small thing. "A walk at lunch." "Listening to music on the commute." "Actually sitting down to eat." The bar is deliberately low because the question is not about optimization — it's about intention. You're planting one small seed of agency before the day's demands take over. The thing you name might not happen. That's fine. The naming is the practice. It reminds you that you have preferences, and that your day is not entirely determined by external forces.

Ninety seconds. Three questions. No equipment, no app required (though an app helps with consistency). The check-in works because it creates a moment of self-awareness before the day's momentum builds. Once the day starts — once the first email is read, the first message is received, the first task is begun — your attention belongs to the world. The morning check-in claims ninety seconds for yourself before that handover happens.

The most common objection: "I don't have time in the morning." You have ninety seconds. The check-in takes less time than brushing your teeth. Less time than making coffee. Less time than scrolling your phone, which is what most people actually do in the first ninety seconds of the morning. The issue isn't time — it's friction. The check-in requires you to pause before reaching for the phone, which is a habit most of us don't have.

The fix: anchor it. Put a note on your phone that says "Check in first." Or set a reminder for two minutes after your alarm. Or leave your journaling app on the home screen and your email on the second page. The goal is to make the check-in the path of least resistance in the first ninety seconds of waking up.

After a week, the check-in becomes automatic. After a month, it becomes essential — you'll notice the days you skip it feel different, slightly more reactive, slightly less intentional. That difference is the value. Ninety seconds of self-awareness, taken before the world asks for your attention, changes the shape of the day. Not dramatically. Not visibly. But enough.

## This Week's Experiment
For seven days, do the three-question morning check-in before you touch your phone. Ninety seconds: how am I feeling, what am I approaching, what would make today slightly better. That's it.
