## Body (600 words)

Twenty weeks ago, we started this newsletter with one idea: wellbeing practices don't need to be big to work. They need to be small enough to survive. Every week since, we've run one experiment — a tiny habit, a reframing technique, a reflection prompt — and invited you to try it.

Here's what twenty weeks of small experiments taught us.

**Small beats big. Consistently.**
Every experiment that asked for a small action — one mood rating, one sentence, one breath — had higher completion rates than any experiment that asked for more. The one-sentence journal entry survived longer than the three-page version. The one-word emotion label stuck better than the emotional analysis exercise. The three-breath reset was used more often than the ten-minute meditation. The pattern is clear: the smaller the ask, the higher the survival rate. This isn't laziness — it's physics. Less friction means more movement.

**The floor carries the practice.**
The people who maintained their habits across all twenty weeks weren't the ones who did the most. They were the ones who had a defined floor — a minimum viable version of each habit that they could do on any day, no matter how bad. The floor is what keeps the practice alive during hard weeks. The ceiling is what makes it meaningful during good ones. You need both, but the floor comes first. Without a floor, the ceiling is irrelevant — you never reach it because you've already stopped.

**Naming changes experiencing.**
The experiments that involved naming — naming emotions, naming what drained you, naming what you're letting go of — consistently produced the most feedback. There's something powerful about putting a precise word on an internal experience. The naming doesn't fix the experience, but it changes your relationship to it. A named emotion is a managed emotion. An unnamed emotion is a ruling emotion.

**Data corrects the narrative.**
The mood tracking experiments revealed, over and over, that memory is unreliable. People remembered bad weeks as worse than they were and forgot good days entirely. The data — even crude data, like a single daily number — corrected the story. Not by being positive, but by being accurate. Accuracy is comforting in a way that reassurance isn't, because accuracy is trustworthy. You can argue with "it'll be fine." You can't argue with your own chart.

**Privacy enables honesty.**
The experiments that involved journaling and reflection only worked when people felt safe. And safety came from knowing the entries were private — stored locally, not on a server, not accessible to anyone else. The privacy isn't a feature. It's a precondition. Without it, the writing softens, the honesty fades, and the practice becomes performance. Local-first isn't a technical choice. It's an emotional one.

**The practice is the point.**
No single experiment changed anyone's life. No single mood entry, journal sentence, or three-breath reset was transformative on its own. But twenty weeks of them — layered, accumulated, maintained through good weeks and bad — created something that no single intervention could: a practice. A sustained, quiet, daily relationship with your own inner life. Not a fix. Not a cure. A practice. And practices, unlike fixes, compound.

If you've been here since Issue 01, you've run twenty experiments. You have twenty weeks of data, reflections, and small habits. That's not a small thing. That's a foundation. Whatever you build next — a deeper meditation practice, a therapy journey, a new habit system — it'll be built on the base of knowing yourself a little better than you did twenty weeks ago.

If you're new here, welcome. The experiments don't expire. Start with Issue 01. Pick one. Make it small. See what happens.

Twenty weeks. One small experiment at a time. That's how it works. That's how it always works.
