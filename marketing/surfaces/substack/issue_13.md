## Body (600 words)

If you do a weekly review at work, you already know the format: what did I accomplish, what's blocked, what's next. It's useful. It keeps projects moving. But it measures only one dimension of your life — output. It tells you nothing about how the week felt, what drained you, or what gave you energy.

The result is a common but invisible problem: you optimize for productivity and wonder why you're burned out. You review your tasks but not your state. You plan your next week but don't check whether last week was sustainable.

The fix is a parallel review — a fifteen-minute weekly check-in designed for mental health, not output. Five sections. Same time each week. Not a replacement for your work review; a companion to it.

**Section 1: Mood Summary (3 minutes)**
Look at the week's mood entries. Identify the high point and the low point. Don't analyze them — just name them. "Tuesday was the high at an 8. Thursday was the low at a 3." This gives you a quick altitude reading: how much range did the week have? Was it relatively stable, or was it a rollercoaster? Over time, you'll see which weeks have wide ranges and what those weeks had in common.

**Section 2: Energy Audit (3 minutes)**
Answer two questions: What drained you this week? What fueled you? One of each, minimum. "The three-hour meeting on Wednesday drained me. The walk on Saturday morning fueled me." This is the most actionable section — it reveals the inputs that affect your wellbeing, which you can then adjust. You can't reduce what drained you if you don't know what it was.

**Section 3: Pattern Noticed (3 minutes)**
Was there a recurring theme across the week? Not a single event — a pattern. "I was more irritable than usual." "I slept poorly most nights." "I felt lonely on the days I worked from home." Patterns become visible only in retrospect. The weekly review is where they surface.

**Section 4: One Intention (3 minutes)**
Not a goal — a quality. Not "exercise three times" but "move more." Not "call two friends" but "connect." The intention is a direction, not a target. It guides the week without dictating specific actions. "More quiet" might mean fewer meetings, or a solo walk, or turning off notifications. The intention sets the direction; you choose the specific moves.

**Section 5: One Thing to Let Go Of (3 minutes)**
Something from this week that you're releasing. A mistake you keep replaying. An awkward interaction. An unfinished task that's nagging at you. Name it, write it down, and set it down. Not forever — just for now. The release prevents emotional accumulation — the buildup of unprocessed moments that eventually weigh you down without knowing why.

Fifteen minutes. Five sections. Once a week. The power is not in any single review — it's in the archive. After twelve weeks, you have twelve data points. You can compare weeks. You can see that the weeks with morning walks had higher mood averages. You can see that the "drained by" items cluster around a specific recurring meeting. You can see that the "let go of" items often relate to a particular relationship.

This is not therapy. It's a weather report. But weather reports, taken weekly, become climate data. And climate data tells you what to pack for the season.

Do it at the same time every week. Friday afternoon, Sunday morning, whenever works. The consistency matters more than the timing. After a month, it'll feel as routine as your work review — but it'll be the one that actually tells you how you're doing.

## This Week's Experiment
Do one fifteen-minute weekly review using the five sections above. Don't worry about doing it perfectly. Just complete all five sections, even if the answers are short.
