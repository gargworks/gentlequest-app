## Body (600 words)

Perfectionism tells you that lowering your standards means giving up. That if you do less than your best, you're failing. That anything worth doing is worth doing perfectly.

This is the single most destructive belief in the wellness space. Not because high standards are bad — they're not — but because standards that are too high to sustain are functionally identical to no standards at all. A workout you never do contributes nothing. A journal entry you never write helps no one. A meditation practice you abandon after a week changes nothing.

The counterintuitive truth: lowering your standards is how you keep going. A fifty-percent effort you actually make beats a hundred-percent effort you skip. Every time.

Let's reframe the language. "Lowering your standards" sounds like decline. What it actually is: right-sizing your expectations to match your current capacity. Capacity isn't fixed — it varies by day, by week, by season. On a good day, your capacity might be 90%. On a bad day, it might be 30%. If your standard is always 100%, you'll hit it on good days and fail on every other day. If your standard is "match the day's capacity," you'll succeed almost every day — at a varying level of effort.

The perfectionist objection: "But if I allow myself to do 30%, I'll always do 30%." This fear is unfounded. In practice, the opposite happens. When you give yourself permission to do less, you do less on the hard days — but you also show up on the hard days, which you wouldn't have otherwise. And on the good days, your natural capacity pulls you back toward higher effort. The standard doesn't cap your ceiling. It raises your floor.

Here's the protocol. Once a week, during your weekly review, audit one habit for standard inflation. Ask: "Is the standard I've set for this habit sustainable on my worst day?" If the answer is no, lower the standard. Not permanently — just to the current floor. A five-prompt reflection becomes one prompt. A page of journaling becomes three sentences. A thirty-minute walk becomes a five-minute walk.

The fear is that lowering the standard means the habit becomes worthless. It doesn't. A five-minute walk still moves your body. Three sentences still maintain the journaling practice. One prompt still closes the mental loop of the day. The value is in the showing up, not in the volume. And showing up at a lower standard is infinitely better than not showing up at all.

The guilt that follows lowering standards is real but manageable. It comes from the gap between the old standard and the new one. The gap feels like failure. It isn't. It's calibration. You're adjusting the tool to fit the hand, not weakening the hand to fit the tool. The habit serves you, not the other way around.

The test of a well-calibrated habit: you can do it on a bad day without resentment. If doing the habit feels like a burden, the standard is too high. If it feels almost too easy, the standard is right. "Too easy" is the goal. "Too easy" is what survives.

This week, pick one habit that you've been struggling to maintain. Cut it in half. Then cut it in half again. Keep cutting until it feels almost laughably small — small enough that you'd do it even on a day you're sick, tired, and running late. That's your new standard. Not forever. Just for now. Just until the habit is so automatic that it starts to grow on its own.

Lowering your standards is not giving up. It's the most sophisticated thing you can do for your habits. It's saying: "I'd rather do this small thing consistently than this big thing never." And that trade — consistency over intensity — is the trade that changes everything over time.

## This Week's Experiment
Pick one habit you've been struggling with. Cut the standard in half. Then in half again. Do the smaller version every day for seven days. Notice if your consistency improves.
