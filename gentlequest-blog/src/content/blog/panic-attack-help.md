A panic attack is not a character flaw. It's not a weakness. It's not something you can "just snap out of."

It's your body's alarm system misfiring — flooding you with adrenaline, making your heart pound, your breath shallow, your thoughts race. Your body is trying to save you from a threat that isn't there.

If you're having a panic attack right now, **stop reading and do this**:

1. Put both feet flat on the floor
2. Name one thing you can see, one thing you can hear, one thing you can feel
3. Breathe out slowly — longer than your breath in
4. Remind yourself: "This will pass. It always does."

If you're not in crisis right now, keep reading. This guide will help you understand what's happening and what to do next.

## What a panic attack actually is

A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear or discomfort that peaks within minutes. The American Psychological Association (APA) identifies these common symptoms:

- Racing or pounding heart
- Sweating
- Trembling or shaking
- Shortness of breath or feeling smothered
- Choking sensation
- Chest pain or discomfort
- Nausea or abdominal distress
- Dizziness or lightheadedness
- Derealization (feeling unreal) or depersonalization (feeling detached from yourself)
- Fear of losing control or going crazy
- Fear of dying
- Numbness or tingling
- Chills or hot flashes

The key word is **sudden**. Panic attacks don't build up gradually — they hit fast and hard. That's what makes them so terrifying: there's often no obvious trigger.

## Why your body does this

Your sympathetic nervous system evolved to save your life. When a tiger jumps out of the bushes, it floods your body with adrenaline so you can fight or run. That's the "fight-or-flight" response.

A panic attack is the same response — but the tiger is imaginary. Your brain misinterprets a bodily sensation (a skipped heartbeat, a shallow breath, a strange thought) as a threat, and triggers the alarm.

Once the alarm sounds, your body reacts: heart rate spikes, breathing quickens, blood shifts away from your extremities (causing tingling or numbness). These sensations are uncomfortable, so your brain interprets them as more evidence of danger. The cycle escalates.

This is called the **panic cycle**, and understanding it is the first step to breaking it.

## What to do during a panic attack

### 1. Don't fight it

Trying to "stop" a panic attack often makes it worse. The harder you fight, the more adrenaline your body produces. Instead, accept that it's happening. Tell yourself: "This is a panic attack. It's uncomfortable, but it's not dangerous. It will end."

### 2. Regulate your breathing

When you panic, your breathing gets shallow and fast. This causes CO2 to drop in your blood, which causes more panic symptoms (dizziness, tingling). Breaking this cycle is critical.

Try **4-7-8 breathing**:
- Breathe in through your nose for 4 counts
- Hold for 7 counts
- Breathe out through your mouth for 8 counts
- Repeat 4 times

The long exhale activates your parasympathetic nervous system — the "rest and digest" response that counteracts fight-or-flight.

### 3. Ground yourself in the present

Panic pulls you into your body and your fear. Grounding techniques pull you back to the external world.

Try the **5-4-3-2-1 technique**:
- Name 5 things you can see
- Name 4 things you can touch
- Name 3 things you can hear
- Name 2 things you can smell
- Name 1 thing you can taste

This forces your brain to process sensory input instead of fear signals.

### 4. Remind yourself it's temporary

Panic attacks typically peak within 10 minutes and subside within 20-30 minutes. Remind yourself: "This has happened before. It ended. This one will end too."

### 5. Don't run

If you leave the situation you're in (a store, a meeting, a car), your brain learns that escaping is the right response. This reinforces the panic. If you can, stay where you are and ride it out.

## How to prevent the next panic attack

### Know your triggers

Panic attacks often have triggers, even if they don't feel like it. Common ones include:

- Caffeine (especially in high doses)
- Sleep deprivation
- Alcohol (especially during withdrawal)
- Stress accumulation
- Specific situations (crowds, driving, enclosed spaces)
- Bodily sensations (a skipped heartbeat, a moment of dizziness)

Track when your attacks happen. Patterns will emerge.

### Practice daily breathing

People who have panic attacks tend to breathe shallowly and chronically hyperventilate. This makes the body more sensitive to CO2 changes, which makes panic more likely.

Practice 4-7-8 breathing for 5 minutes daily — not during an attack, but when you're calm. This trains your nervous system to shift into parasympathetic mode more easily.

### Learn your early warning signs

Most panic attacks have a "ramp-up" period before they peak. Learn to recognize yours:

- A slight increase in heart rate
- A feeling of unease
- Shallow breathing
- A "something's wrong" thought

If you catch these early, you can intervene before the full attack hits.

### Consider professional support

If you're having panic attacks regularly, you may have panic disorder. The good news: panic disorder is highly treatable. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) has a strong evidence base for panic disorder, and medications (SSRIs) can also help.

**If you don't have access to therapy right now**, self-help resources can make a difference. The key skills — breathing regulation, cognitive restructuring, interoceptive exposure — can be practiced on your own.

## When to seek immediate help

A panic attack can feel like a heart attack. If you're not sure which one you're having, **get medical help**. It's always better to be safe.

Call 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) if:
- You're having thoughts of self-harm
- You feel like you might hurt yourself
- You need someone to talk to right now

988 is free, confidential, and available 24/7. You don't have to be suicidal to call — they help with panic attacks too.
