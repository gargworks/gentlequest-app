Most mental health apps bury the safety plan.

It's in Settings. Under a submenu. Three taps deep. Behind "More." Next to "Export data" and "About."

The logic is understandable — you don't want to scare new users. A safety plan feels heavy. It implies crisis. So apps tuck it away, hoping you'll fill it in during a calm moment and never need it.

But that logic breaks down in the moment you actually need it.

## The problem with buried safety plans

When you're in crisis, you're not navigating menus. You're not reading labels. Your working memory is shot. Your hands might be shaking. You need the thing that helps — right now, not in three taps.

A safety plan that takes 4 taps to reach is a safety plan that doesn't get used.

Research on crisis intervention consistently shows that **reducing friction** is the single most effective design choice. The fewer steps between a person and help, the more likely they are to get help. This is why suicide prevention hotlines are 3 digits (988 in the US), not 10.

A 2021 study in the Journal of Affective Disorders found that people who had immediate access to their safety plan were **2.5x more likely to use it** during a crisis than those who had to navigate to find it. The difference between one tap and four taps is the difference between reaching out and not.

## What is a safety plan?

A safety plan is a personalized, written plan that helps you get through a crisis. It's not a replacement for professional help — it's a bridge to help when you can't think clearly.

The concept was developed by Dr. Barbara Stanley and Dr. Gregory Brown, and it's been shown to reduce suicidal ideation and increase treatment engagement. The Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention is now used in hospitals, schools, and crisis centers worldwide.

A good safety plan includes:

1. **Warning signs** — what to watch for in yourself (thoughts, feelings, behaviors, situations)
2. **Internal coping strategies** — things you can do alone to take your mind off the crisis
3. **Social contacts** — people and places that can distract you from the crisis
4. **People you trust** — friends or family members you can ask for help
5. **Professionals and agencies** — your therapist, crisis lines, emergency services
6. **Making the environment safe** — removing or securing things that could be used to harm yourself

## How to create your own safety plan

You don't need an app to create a safety plan. You can write one on paper, in a notes app, or in GentleQuest. The important thing is that you do it **before** you need it.

### Step 1: Know your warning signs

What happens in your body, mind, or life when you're heading toward a crisis? Common warning signs include:
- Withdrawing from friends or family
- Difficulty sleeping or sleeping too much
- Intense mood swings
- Feeling trapped or like a burden
- Increased substance use
- Giving away possessions
- Thinking about death or dying

Write down your top 3-5 warning signs. These are your early warning system.

### Step 2: List your coping strategies

What helps you feel better when you're struggling? These should be things you can do **alone**, without needing anyone else:
- Deep breathing exercises
- Going for a walk
- Listening to a specific playlist
- Drawing or journaling
- Holding something cold (ice, cold water on your face)
- The 5-4-3-2-1 grounding technique

### Step 3: Identify your people

Who can you reach out to? List 2-3 people you trust, with their phone numbers. Be specific — don't just write "my sister," write "Sarah — 555-0123."

Also list places you can go where you'll be around other people:
- A specific coffee shop
- A park
- A library
- A community center

### Step 4: Add professional contacts

- Your therapist or counselor (if you have one)
- 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the US — call or text)
- Crisis Text Line (text HOME to 741741)
- Your local emergency number (911 in the US)
- Your doctor's office

### Step 5: Make your environment safe

If you're in crisis, remove or secure things that could be used to harm yourself. This might mean:
- Asking a friend to hold onto medications
- Removing firearms from the home
- Securing sharp objects
- Having someone stay with you

## Fill it when you're calm. Find it when you're not.

That's the whole principle. The safety plan is not a feature you use every day. It's a feature that exists for the worst day. And on the worst day, you don't have the bandwidth to dig.

If you haven't filled one in yet, take 2 minutes now. Open the app → tap your profile → tap "Safety Plan." Add one crisis number. Add one person. That's enough to start.

You can always add more later. But having something — even one number — already there when you need it is the difference between reaching out and not.
