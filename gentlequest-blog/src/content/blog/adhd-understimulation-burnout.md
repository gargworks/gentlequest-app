## The burnout paradox

If you have ADHD, you've probably experienced this: you're burned out, so you try to rest. You lie in bed. You do nothing. And instead of feeling better, you feel *worse*.

More anxious. More restless. More exhausted. More... stuck.

If typical burnout advice (rest, do nothing, take a break) makes you feel worse, you're not doing it wrong. You might be experiencing **understimulation burnout** — a type of burnout that's specific to ADHD brains, and that requires a completely different recovery approach.

## What is understimulation burnout?

Typical burnout is caused by **overstimulation** — too much work, too much stress, too many demands. The treatment is rest and reduction.

Understimulation burnout is caused by **too little stimulation** — not enough novelty, challenge, or engagement. The ADHD brain craves dopamine, and dopamine comes from novelty, challenge, and interest. When you remove all stimulation (by "resting"), the ADHD brain starves.

The result is a paradox: the thing that helps neurotypical burnout (rest) makes ADHD burnout worse.

## Signs of understimulation burnout

- Resting makes you feel more anxious, not less
- You feel exhausted but can't sleep
- You're bored but can't engage with anything
- Simple tasks feel impossible (not because they're hard, but because they're *uninteresting*)
- You crave novelty — new apps, new projects, new rabbit holes
- You feel "stuck" in a way that rest doesn't fix
- You oscillate between "too tired to do anything" and "can't stop doing something"

## Why typical burnout advice backfires

Standard burnout advice says:
- Take a break
- Do nothing
- Rest
- Reduce stimulation

For an ADHD brain, this is the *opposite* of what helps. The ADHD brain needs *the right kind* of stimulation — not less stimulation. Removing all stimulation creates a dopamine deficit that feels like depression, anxiety, and exhaustion combined.

## What actually helps: gentle stimulation

The recovery for understimulation burnout isn't rest — it's **gentle, low-stakes stimulation**. Not a new project (that's a dopamine chase that'll crash). Not rest (that's starvation). Something in between.

### 1. Micro-novelty

Small, low-stakes new experiences:
- Take a different route on a walk
- Try a new tea or coffee
- Listen to a new genre of music
- Rearrange one thing on your desk

The key is *low stakes*. You're not starting a new project. You're just giving your brain a small hit of novelty.

### 2. Tiny quests

2-5 minute activities that have a clear beginning and end:
- Do one stretch
- Name three things you can see
- Take five deep breaths with a focus on the exhale
- Write one sentence about how you feel

These are small enough to not trigger overwhelm, but structured enough to give the ADHD brain a sense of completion (and a small dopamine hit).

### 3. Body-based engagement

When your brain won't engage, your body can:
- Cold water on your face (activates the dive reflex, resets the nervous system)
- A short walk outside (movement + novelty + light)
- Pushing against a wall (proprioceptive input)
- Humming or singing (vagus nerve stimulation)

### 4. Interest-based engagement (not obligation)

Do something interesting, not something you "should" do:
- Watch a documentary on a topic you're curious about
- Take apart something and put it back together
- Draw, doodle, or make something with your hands
- Go down a Wikipedia rabbit hole (time-boxed)

The goal isn't productivity. It's giving your brain the stimulation it needs to recover.

## The trap to avoid: dopamine chasing

The risk with understimulation burnout is that you swing from "doing nothing" to "doing everything" — starting three new projects, buying new tools, planning a life overhaul. This is a dopamine chase, and it'll crash in 2-3 days, leaving you more burned out than before.

The antidote is *gentle* stimulation. Not a sprint. Not a marathon. A walk.
