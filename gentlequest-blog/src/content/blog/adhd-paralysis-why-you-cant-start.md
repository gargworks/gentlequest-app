## You're not lazy. You're frozen.

You know the feeling. There's something you need to do. It's not even hard. Maybe it's sending one email. Maybe it's putting dishes in the sink.

And you just... can't.

You sit there. You scroll. You feel the guilt building. You tell yourself "just do it." You don't. The guilt gets heavier. The task gets harder. The cycle continues.

This isn't laziness. It's **ADHD paralysis** — and it's one of the most common, most misunderstood symptoms of ADHD.

## What's actually happening in your brain

ADHD paralysis is an **executive function crash**. Your brain's task-management system — the prefrontal cortex — gets overwhelmed and shuts down. It's like a computer freezing when too many programs are open.

The freeze happens because your brain can't decide:
- **What** to do first
- **How** to start
- **Whether** it's worth the effort
- **When** to do it

All four questions fire at once, none gets answered, and the system hangs.

This is why "just do it" doesn't work. You're not choosing not to do the thing. Your brain literally cannot initiate the action.

## 3 ways to break the freeze (without shame)

### 1. The "one spoon" method

Don't clean the kitchen. Clean **one spoon**.

Not one dish. One spoon. Wash it. Put it away. Done.

The trick is making the task so small that your brain's task-evaluation system doesn't have time to freeze. One spoon doesn't trigger the "this is too much" response. One spoon is easy. One spoon is done in 10 seconds.

And here's the secret: once you've washed one spoon, washing a second one feels easier. Not because you've "built momentum" — but because you've already started, so the initiation problem is solved.

### 2. The 5-minute timer

Set a timer for 5 minutes. Tell yourself you only have to do the task for 5 minutes. When the timer goes off, you can stop.

This works because it removes the "how long will this take?" question — one of the four questions that causes the freeze. 5 minutes is a known, bounded amount of time. Your brain can commit to 5 minutes.

Most of the time, you'll keep going after the timer. Not always. That's fine. 5 minutes of a task is infinitely more than 0 minutes.

### 3. Body doubling (without a person)

ADHD brains often start tasks more easily when someone else is present. It's called "body doubling." But you don't need an actual person.

Put on a YouTube video of someone doing a similar task. "Clean with me" videos. "Study with me" videos. Even a timer video with gentle background noise.

The presence of another person (even a recorded one) signals to your brain that "this is a thing people are doing right now" — which makes starting feel safer.

## What doesn't work

- **Guilt.** Guilt makes paralysis worse, not better. It adds emotional weight to a system that's already overloaded.
- **Streaks.** Streak counters turn each day you don't do the thing into a visible failure. For ADHD brains, this creates avoidance, not motivation.
- **"Just try harder."** This is like telling someone with a broken leg to "just walk harder." The capability isn't there, and willpower doesn't fix executive dysfunction.

## The gentle path

ADHD paralysis is a neurological event, not a character flaw. Your brain's initiation system has a different operating system. It needs different tools — not more willpower.

The next time you're frozen:
1. Pick the smallest possible version of the task (one spoon)
2. Set a 5-minute timer
3. Put on a body-doubling video

And if none of that works today, that's okay. Your brain is telling you it's overloaded. Listen to it. Come back tomorrow.
