## 2 AM. Again.

The room is dark. Everyone else is asleep. And your brain has decided this is the perfect time to:
- Replay that embarrassing thing you said in 2019
- Worry about something that might happen three months from now
- Plan a conversation you'll never have
- List everything you should have done today

You're not alone. **Night anxiety** is one of the most common forms of anxiety — and one of the most exhausting. It's not just "can't sleep." It's "can't stop thinking long enough to try."

## Why anxiety gets worse at night

During the day, your brain is busy. Work, conversations, decisions, notifications — there's always something to focus on. Your anxiety doesn't disappear, but it has competition.

At night, the competition disappears. There's nothing to distract your brain from its own thoughts. Every worry you suppressed during the day comes back. And now there's nowhere to run.

There's also a biological reason: **cortisol levels fluctuate**. For some people, cortisol (the stress hormone) spikes in the early morning hours — around 2-4 AM. This can wake you up with a jolt of physical anxiety: racing heart, tight chest, mind racing.

Your brain interprets this physical sensation as "something is wrong" — and starts generating worries to explain the feeling. The worries aren't the cause. They're the brain's attempt to make sense of the physical sensation.

## 4 things that actually help at 2 AM

### 1. The 4-7-8 breath

This is the one that works when nothing else does. It's a specific breathing pattern that activates your parasympathetic nervous system (the "rest and digest" mode).

- Breathe in through your nose for **4 seconds**
- Hold your breath for **7 seconds**
- Breathe out through your mouth for **8 seconds**

Do this 4 times. The long exhale is the key — it forces your heart rate to slow down, which signals to your brain that the threat has passed.

Don't worry about doing it "right." Just make the exhale longer than the inhale. That's the mechanism.

### 2. The "worry list" dump

Your brain is keeping you awake because it's trying to not forget something. So let it forget safely.

Turn on a dim light. Get a piece of paper. Write down every worry in your head. Not solutions. Just the worries.

"I'm worried about the meeting on Thursday."
"I'm worried I said the wrong thing to Sarah."
"I'm worried about money."

Once they're on paper, your brain can let go of them. It was holding on because it was afraid of losing them. The paper is a better container than your working memory.

### 3. The 5-4-3-2-1 grounding

When your mind is racing, pull it back to your body:

- **5** things you can see (the ceiling, the shadow of the curtain, the glow of a light)
- **4** things you can feel (the sheet, the pillow, the mattress, the air)
- **3** things you can hear (the hum of the fridge, a distant car, your breath)
- **2** things you can smell (laundry, the air, nothing)
- **1** thing you can taste (the inside of your mouth)

This isn't distraction. It's **redirecting** your brain from the internal loop to external reality. The present moment is almost always safer than your brain thinks it is at 2 AM.

### 4. Get up (yes, really)

If you've been lying awake for more than 20 minutes, staying in bed is making it worse. Your brain starts associating the bed with anxiety, not sleep.

Get up. Go to another room. Do something boring — read a physical book (not a screen), fold some laundry, sit in a chair and do the 4-7-8 breath.

When you feel sleepy, go back to bed. This breaks the "bed = anxiety" association.

## What to avoid

- **Your phone.** The blue light suppresses melatonin, and the content (even "relaxing" content) gives your brain something to process. It's the opposite of what you need.
- **Dooring.** "Dooring" is when you lie in bed thinking about how you can't sleep, which makes you more anxious, which makes sleep harder. The solution is getting up (see above).
- **Caffeine after 2 PM.** Caffeine has a half-life of 5-6 hours. A 3 PM coffee means half the caffeine is still in your system at 9 PM. And anxiety + caffeine = 2 AM racing thoughts.

## The thing to remember

Night anxiety feels like it will last forever. It won't. Your brain's cortisol cycle will shift. The sun will come up. The worries that felt catastrophic at 2 AM will feel smaller at 8 AM.

You're not broken. Your brain is doing what brains do — trying to protect you from threats, just at the wrong time. Be gentle with it.
